# Coffee shop waste reduction: a practical guide

**TL;DR:** Most coffee shop waste never reaches a customer. It sits in the pastry case until close, goes down the drain as over-steamed milk, or leaves as grounds in a bin bag. Log every discard by reason for two weeks, enforce FIFO in the pastry case, portion with scales and marked scoops, and give unsold product a second life before it hits the bin. A spreadsheet handles the tracking at first. Once you are logging dozens of discards a week, a kitchen ops app like [KitchFlow](/) makes the waste log automatic.

If you already run a tight ship on inventory and staffing, waste is usually the last leak left in your margin. It is also the one owners notice last, because it disappears a little at a time instead of all at once.

This guide is for cafe owners and managers who already have the basics in place and want to find the waste that is still costing them.

## Why coffee shop waste is different from restaurant waste

Most restaurant-waste advice is written for kitchens with a la carte menus and long ticket times, where the biggest category is plate waste, meaning food a customer ordered and did not finish. Across foodservice, [ReFED](https://refed.org/food-waste/the-problem/) puts plate waste and unsold product at roughly 70% of the sector's food waste, and values food surplus across the whole food industry at $240 billion in 2024.

A cafe's profile looks different. There is very little plate waste in the traditional sense, because most orders are drinks and grab-and-go items. The loss shows up somewhere else: unsold pastries at closing, spoiled dairy, over-steamed or dumped milk, mis-timed espresso shots, and grounds that go straight in the trash. Apply general restaurant advice to a cafe and you are solving for the wrong 70%.

## The four sources of waste (and what each one tells you)

Use four tags to log every discard, the same way you would in any kitchen. The categories point to different fixes in a cafe.

**Spoilage.** Expired milk, stale pastry. Points to an ordering or rotation problem.

**Prep error.** Burnt espresso shot, over-steamed milk, wrong recipe. Points to a training or standardization problem.

**Overproduction.** Pastries baked or ordered in that do not sell by close. Points to a forecasting or display problem.

**Customer return.** A drink remade because it was wrong. Points to a POS or communication problem.

Track for two weeks before changing anything. In a cafe, overproduction and spoilage usually account for most of it. That is good news, because both are fixable with scheduling and ordering changes rather than new equipment.

## Fix the pastry case first: FIFO and markdowns

The pastry case is where cafe margin quietly leaks out. New product looks better in front, so staff put it there, and yesterday's stock sits in back until it is thrown out at close.

The fix is the rule every kitchen skips under pressure: **new stock goes to the back, older stock moves to the front.** Pair it with a same-day markdown rule. Anything unsold by mid-afternoon gets discounted, repurposed into a staff meal or bread pudding, or donated, rather than sitting until close and going straight in the bin.

This is the cheapest fix on the list. It needs no new tools, just a posted rule and one person checking it each shift.

## Portion control: scales beat guesswork

Milk is easy to over-pour and easy to over-steam, and dry goods like syrups and toppings are easy to over-scoop. A digital scale at the bar and marked scoops for dry ingredients cut waste immediately, because they remove the guesswork that causes both waste and inconsistent drinks.

If your food cost is running a few points over target, portioning is usually cheaper to fix than menu or pricing changes. A scale and a set of scoops is a training and equipment cost, not a strategy overhaul.

## Match orders to actual demand

Most cafe over-ordering comes from ordering perishables (milk, cream, pastry ingredients) on the same cadence as non-perishables (cups, lids, syrups). Split them.

- **Non-perishables.** Order in bulk on a longer cycle. You are not racing a shelf-life clock.
- **Perishables.** Order smaller and more often, sized to your actual par level, meaning peak daily usage plus supplier lead time, rather than a round-number guess.
- **Local and seasonal where possible.** Shorter supply chains usually mean fresher product with more usable shelf life once it arrives.

[MarketMan](https://www.marketman.com/blog/ways-to-reduce-food-waste-in-your-coffee-chain) makes the same point about supplier collaboration and standardized recipes across coffee operations. The cafe-specific twist is treating dairy and pastry inputs as a separate, tighter-cycle category from everything else in the stockroom.

## Give unsold product a second life

Before anything hits the bin, it should pass a second-life check. Can it be marked down, turned into a staff meal or a new item like day-old croissants into bread pudding, or donated to a local food-rescue program? Build that check into the closing checklist instead of leaving it to whoever is on shift to remember. That is what makes it happen every day rather than once.

## Coffee grounds: the waste stream most cafes ignore

Every shot leaves spent grounds behind, and most cafes bag them with the regular trash. Spent grounds are compostable, and for cafes near urban gardens or community compost programs they are often wanted for free. Some set out a free-grounds bin for gardening customers. Others partner with a local composting service.

The cost saving is small. The effort is close to zero once the bin is in place, and it is visible enough that customers notice.

## Tools: spreadsheet vs. a dedicated app

## Your 14-day waste log

**Week 1.** Log every discard with a reason tag, even on paper if that is fastest. Change nothing else yet. Post the FIFO rule at the pastry case.

**Week 2.** Review the tags for repeat offenders. Set a markdown time, say 3pm, for unsold pastry. Add scales or marked scoops if portioning shows up as a pattern. Set up a compost or grounds-reuse bin if you do not have one.

## FAQ

**How much food does a typical coffee shop waste?**
There is no reliable cafe-specific benchmark yet. Across foodservice, ReFED's research points to unsold product and plate waste making up around 70% of the sector's food waste. For a cafe, that shows up mostly as unsold pastry and spoiled dairy rather than plate waste.

**What is the fastest way to cut coffee shop waste?**
Enforce FIFO in the pastry case with a same-day markdown rule. It needs no new equipment, just a posted rule and a check each shift, and it targets the biggest single loss point in most cafes.

**Are coffee grounds actually worth composting?**
The cost saving is small, but the effort is close to zero once you have a bin. It is real, visible waste diversion that customers notice, especially near community gardens.

**Do I need software to track cafe waste?**
No. A spreadsheet works for a small team. Move to a dedicated tool when manual logging starts slipping because of volume, not before.
